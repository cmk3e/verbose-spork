Briefing: Stack-Size Adjustments Through the Tournament Phases

Summary of two PokerCoaching videos (Matt Affleck's webinar on adjusting ranges to stack depth, and Jonathan Little's video on bubble strategy), with a comparison to the charts and solves in the Postflop Framework Drills. November 2026.

Summary

The webinar's thesis: as effective stacks get shorter, you play about the same number of hands but different ones. You swap small pairs, small suited connectors and wheel aces for offsuit Broadways and bigger suited aces, and you stack off with top pair far more readily. The presenter calls not making this shift one of the biggest and easiest-to-fix mistakes weaker tournament players make.

The five ideas that carry the rest:

  • Deep (150bb+), play for straights, flushes and sets. Suitedness and connectedness matter most, and one pair is rarely good enough to play a 300bb pot.
  • At 50bb, play for strong top pairs. Offsuit Broadways (KJo, KTo, ATo, A9o) gain value; 22–44, 54s/65s and A2s–A4s lose it.
  • Your open percentage barely changes. A middle-position range goes from about 24% at 150bb to about 23% at 50bb; only the hand types change.
  • SPR drives everything. Below an SPR of about 5, good top pairs can stack off and normal bet sizes get the money in by the river. Above 5, they can't, and you need a stronger hand.
  • Use effective stacks, not your own stack. With 100bb against a table of 50bb stacks you are playing a 50bb game; with 40bb against 20bb stacks you are playing 20bb.

Scope: the main webinar is about stack depth, not tournament stages, and barely covers the bubble. A second PokerCoaching video, Jonathan Little on bubble strategy, fills that gap: ICM, risk premium, how each stack size should play, and 22 hand examples. It has its own section below. Neither video gives push/fold ranges.

Stack-depth bands

There is an inverse relationship: as stacks shrink, big offsuit cards gain value; as stacks grow, suited connectors and small pairs gain value. The webinar uses four bands.

Why hands like KJo improve at 50bb: one pair is never more likely to improve than another (AK and 32 make two pair equally often), but KQ on K-8-4 becomes a hand you can comfortably get all in with at 50bb, while at 150bb you need much more than top pair to stack off.

The middle-position example on the slides: going from 150bb to 50bb removes 70 combos (5.3% of hands: small pairs, small suited connectors, wheel aces) and adds 60 combos of offsuit Broadways, because offsuit hands have 12 combos to a suited hand's 4. The range drops by less than 1%: about 24% at 150bb to about 23% at 50bb.

A 3-bet pot at 100bb plays like a single-raised pot at 50bb. Open to 3bb, 3-bet to 10bb and call: the pot is about 20bb with about 90bb behind, an SPR of about 4.5.

3-betting and bet sizes by stack depth

The 3-bet bluffs change completely between 150bb and 50bb, and the value range gets wider as stacks shrink. The webinar's examples are a button 3-bet against a middle-position open.

150bb50bb
ValueJJ+, AK, AQs (about 44 combos)Wider: adds TT, AQo, AQs, AJs, KQs
BluffsSmall suited connectors and one-gappers (65s, 76s, 87s, 97s, 86s, 75s, T9s) and wheel aces (A5s, A4s), about 36 combosMid suited aces (A9s–A6s, plus A5s) and offsuit Broadways (AJo, KJo, ATo, KTo); also K9s, Q9s, J9s, T9s

3-bet size out of position4–4.5×3.5–4×

Why 3-bet KTo but flat KQo at 50bb. A 3-bet bluff works by folding out better hands. KTo folds out KJ and often KQ (hands with about 70% equity against it), and ATo folds out AJ. KQo folds out almost nothing better, since AK and AQ never fold, and it folds out the KJ and KT you dominate. So flat KQo and keep those dominated hands in.

Why AQ and AJ become value 3-bets at 50bb. Most players jam AK over a 50bb 3-bet, so when they just call, AK is unlikely. AQ then plays almost face up as the best ace on A-high boards. At 150bb they may flat AK, so AQ has to be more careful.

Why A5s over A2s. A5s has more equity when called and blocks 56s on wheel boards; A2s and A3s add too many bluffs with less equity.

Facing a 4-bet jam at 50bb against typical small-stakes players, 3-bet the wider range but fold AQo, AQs, AJs and KQs to the jam: these players 4-bet only their best hands. Against aggressive players who 4-bet jam A5s or 55, call more of them, or 3-bet and call with them.

Kings deep. At 150–200bb it's common to flat 3-bets with KK, since aces are close to the only hand that wants to stack off. Stop stacking off kings against opponents who never 4-bet or 5-bet bluff. That's an opponent read, not a stack-size rule.

Bet sizing postflop follows the same logic. Deep, getting stacks in by the river takes pot-sized bets on every street (or more). At a low SPR, about ⅓ pot on the flop, ½ pot on the turn and a ⅔-pot river jam already gets it all in.

Opening sizes (presenter's own deep-stack approach). Min-raise or raise small under the gun, where you'll be out of position in a deep pot, and raise bigger (about 3×, even 4× early in a tournament) from the cutoff and button, where you'll be in position.

Facing big opens (to 10bb at 100–250bb, common in small live games). Mostly flat, even with QQ or JJ: the open already isolates. Call tighter, folding 66–44 and small suited connectors, and 3-bet only against players who will stack off light.

Opponent adjustments. These ranges are a baseline. Against players who never fold to 3-bets (common in $1 online tournaments), drop the bluffs and 3-bet a wider value range. Against tight openers, 3-bet less; against loose ones, 3-bet more. If there is a cold caller between you and the opener, tighten your 3-bets.

Effective stacks, short stacks and multiway spots

Always size your strategy to the effective stack, the smaller of the stacks involved, not your own. This was the most-asked question in the Q&A.

  • Big stack at a short table. With 100bb against 45–50bb stacks, choose hands as if you have 50bb. With 40bb against 20bb stacks, play like 20bb and tighten up.
  • Don't bully just because you cover everyone. Keep fundamentals and protect your chips. Open wider to pressure stacks only in ICM spots with big pay jumps, or against players who are folding too much.
  • Short stacks behind you (20–25bb) that keep jamming over opens. Open tighter. Prefer hands that block their re-jam range: KJo over a hand like 76s, and an offsuit ace such as A4o gains value because the ace blocks their suited aces and big aces. With short stacks behind, blockers matter more than playability.
  • When to start push/fold. The presenter starts open-jamming some hands at about 15bb, still min-raises a lot at 15bb, and does essentially no jamming above 15bb because he thinks his postflop edge is worth keeping.
  • Calling a 20bb shove. With 40bb or 100bb, call almost the same range; only slightly tighter with the deeper stack because more is at risk.
  • Facing a 10bb under-the-gun jam with 66 in middle position (at 50bb). Fold: 66 is ahead of the jamming range, but five players are left to act. From the small or big blind, closing the action, call.

Small pairs at 50bb. Don't call 3-bets to set-mine; the implied odds aren't there. Fold small pairs early, even up to about 66, when facing an early open with several players left to act who could squeeze. Call them when you close the action in the big blind. Deep, 22–44 can call 3-bets to set-mine, but watch out for set over set at 200bb.

Limpers deep. At 150bb with two early limpers, over-limp 44 rather than isolate: it doesn't block their calling range and hates most flops, so take a cheap flop.

Multiway pots have a lower SPR, so treat them as shallower: strong top pairs gain value, and it's easier to get the money in.

Cash games follow the same principles at the same depths. The webinar's ranges assume antes, so adjust for cash games without antes. The single big-blind ante changes strategy less than people think; it mainly speeds the game up.

Bubble strategy, ICM and risk premium

On the bubble, chips you lose are worth more than chips you win, so you need extra equity, the "risk premium", to call or make an all-in. This section comes from a second PokerCoaching video, Jonathan Little's "Mastering the fundamentals of bubble strategy". The bubble here means you're one or a few eliminations from the money, close enough that you could reasonably fold your way in.

Avoid going broke, but don't lock down. Collecting free equity as others bust is real money. But don't blind out by folding everything: many middle and short stacks tighten too early (say with 150 left and 100 paid), drift from 35bb to 20bb, min-cash, and wreck their chance of a deep run. A min-cash is about 1.5 buy-ins, not a big success.

Know the payout structure. Care about a pay jump only when it's meaningful: in the money with $150 locked, a jump to $156 doesn't matter, but a jump to $250 does. Top-heavy payouts (first used to get about 33% of the prize pool; now nearer 15%) reward gambling to win. Flatter modern payouts reward hanging on and moving up. Small local events are often steep.

Why it costs to gamble (ICM example). Four players each hold 25% of the chips; payouts are $1,000 / $700 / $400 / $200, so each stack is worth $575 on average.

Outcome of a 50/50 all-in between two of themYour ICM equity
Before the hand$575
You win (double up)$800
You lose (out in 4th)$200
Average of win and lose$500

Both players put in $575 of equity ($1,150 total) and get back $1,000 on average. The missing $150 goes to the two players who folded.

Every stack in the tournament matters, which is why there are no generic bubble charts. With 5bb when the average is 25bb, you can fold into the money if 300 are left and 299 get paid, or if several players have 1bb. If nine are left, everyone else has 20bb+ and you have 5bb, nobody else is likely to bust first: you'll have to double up or bubble.

Risk premium

When facing (or making) a bet for all or most of your chips, required equity = the pot-odds equity + a risk premium. If you lose you're out or crippled; if you win you don't double your equity. The premium depends on your stack and your opponent's, and applies when you bluff as well as when you call. The video's rough guide, for facing an all-in or near all-in at a final table or on the bubble:

You vs the bettorExtra equity needed
Short stack vs big stack (you're out if you lose)About 9–15%
Short vs medium, short vs shortA little less than short vs big
Big stack vs big stack (both comfortably cashing)About 18–35%
Big stack vs short stackVery little: losing barely dents you

It can be much higher: with a 1bb stack in the tournament on the bubble, a short stack might need around 70% equity to call. Example: two 50bb stacks when the average is 20bb; QQ facing an all-in from AK is about 50%, but it needs about 70% after the risk premium, which is almost impossible preflop. So fold.

Worked example. Folded to a 40bb big stack in the small blind; you have 10bb in the big blind, with no tiny stacks around. The big stack should shove any two cards, because your risk premium is so large. You call 9bb to win 21bb, so 9 ÷ 21 = 42.8% without ICM. Add about 12% for the bubble and a margin for profit: you need about 56%. Against a 100% shoving range that means about 44+, A6s+, A8o+, K6s+, K8o+, Q8s+ and JTs. Call tighter still (about 99+, KJs+, ATo+) with shorter stacks elsewhere or when you're likely to cash anyway. If you're the obvious short stack and the next to bust, you can call a bit wider.

Opponents differ. Some players ignore ICM and play to win; others play very tight to cash. If an opponent defends loosely, tighten up (you don't want action); if they defend too tightly, loosen up. The presenter once ran over a final-table player who needed 4th place to pay off a house, then called him lighter (A10) the moment that player jammed once 4th was locked.

Postflop on the bubble

  • Big stack: c-bet and lead far more often on boards that suit your range, usually with small bets: each bet nudges a medium stack closer to all in, and by the river their whole stack is at risk.
  • Triple barrel more when the board runs out badly for them, for example a river that completes a straight they can't hold. Bluff catchers can't call profitably once the risk premium is included.
  • Short stack: check-raise less, especially as a bluff; make bigger river folds even against bluffers; get out of pots early rather than reaching big river decisions.
  • Go broke only with a clear edge (for example KK against a loose player who has lots of Ax), or when you're clearly the next to bust.

Stack-to-pot ratio (SPR)

SPR is the effective stack behind divided by the pot on the flop, and it decides how strong a hand must be to stack off. The rule of thumb: below about 5, good top pairs are strong enough to get all in; above 5, be cautious with one pair.

SPR = effective stack behind ÷ pot on the flop

The webinar's two baseline examples (with a 1bb ante):

  • 150bb, early-position 3× open, button calls: 8.5bb pot, 147bb behind, SPR about 17.
  • 50bb, 2.5× open, button calls: 7.5bb pot, 47.5bb behind, SPR about 6.3.

Worked example: KQo opens in middle position, the button calls, flop Q-8-4 rainbow. You bet 5bb on the flop and 12.5bb on the 7♦ turn, and both get called. The river is the 9♥.

Point in the hand40bb deep: pot / behind / SPR100bb deep: pot / behind / SPR
Flop7.5 / 37.5 / 57.5 / 97.5 / 13

ResultJam the river for half pot; KQ is close to the top of a range that would have raised AQ, sets and two pairs earlierCan't get stacks in without overbetting; the caller's range is stronger because AQ, sets, two pairs and straights (JTs) could all just call

Why the same hand is stronger shallow: at low SPR, opponents get their best hands in early (AQ raises or jams, sets and two pairs raise before a scary card), so the hands that just call down are weaker. Deep, they flat more strong hands and float more draws, so the calling range is stronger by the river.

Floating and drawing. Below an SPR of 5, calling a flop bet with a backdoor draw (say A♦2♦) is poor: if you miss, there's no room left to bluff the river. Above 5, there's enough behind to float, pick up draws and represent hands like JT, 56 or 98 on later streets.

Ranges are easier to read shallow. Hands like Q9o or JTo rarely call preflop at 50bb but may at 100bb, so deeper ranges hold more random two pairs and floats.

What to practise.

  • Range viewer: open LJ or HJ and switch between 80, 50, 30 and 20bb to watch the small pairs and suited connectors leave and the offsuit Broadways arrive. Do the same for BTN vs LJ to see the 3-bet bluffs change.
  • Preflop trainer: drill one depth at a time (50bb, then 30bb), with spot types limited to opens and facing a 3-bet.
  • 12bb push/fold: for the short-stack end the webinar skips.
  • Solver flop drill: compare BTN vs BB at 50, 30 and 20bb, and the 3-bet pots, to see SPR change c-bet sizing and stack-off thresholds.

On the bubble, your charts and drills need adjusting. Your PokerCoaching charts, the 12bb push/fold page and the solver spots all assume chip EV, meaning no payout jumps. So on the bubble, call jams tighter than the charts by the risk premium. K7o facing an 11bb small-blind jam, normally a call, becomes a fold. Big stacks can open and jam wider than the charts against opponents who must play tight. The drills don't model ICM, so use the bubble section above as the adjustment layer on top of them.
